Age and Emotion Effects on Human Detection of AI-Cloned Voices

The Problem

For my AP Research project, I investigated whether participants' abilities to distinguish human voices from AI-cloned speech are affected by the participant's age and the emotion of the voice. I chose to explore this topic because voice clones are becoming increasingly convincing, and they are being used for impersonation and scams. I wanted to find out if listeners of different ages responded differently to emotional speech and whether some emotions made identifying AI voices more difficult. This research project connected my interest in artificial intelligence with questions about cybersecurity and how people judge information they hear on the internet.

My Research Process

I designed a quantitative listening study that compared three age groups: 13-25, 26-50, and 51-75. I had 68 participants and got 544 responses with eight different audio clips. The clips included human speech and AI-generated speech that expressed happiness, sadness, anger, or a neutral emotion. Participants identified each clip as either a human voice or an AI-generated voice and rated their confidence from one to five.

The recordings all used the same speaker and sentence to control differences between audio clips. The listening procedure also had volume normalization and a randomized order for the audio clips. I put the collected data into an Excel spreadsheet and analyzed the accuracy and confidence across the age groups and emotions. I analyzed the data using descriptive comparisons, chi-square tests, and a logistic regression model to examine if the patterns found were statistically significant.

Results and Limitations

The youngest age group had the highest average accuracy with 60.3%, followed by the middle group with 56.5% and the oldest group with 51.0%. Participants were most accurate on happy audio clips with an average accuracy of 60.3% and neutral audio clips with an average accuracy of 59.6%. The accuracy was lower for sad clips at 52.2% and angry clips at 51.5%.

After performing the statistical tests, I concluded that the patterns were not statistically significant. The differences show patterns within the sample, but they do not assert that younger people are better than older people at identifying AI voices or that any emotions affect detection.

The study had some limitations. The participants all came from a small geographic area, and the listening task used one speaker and sentence and a small number of clips. These limitations controlled the study but limited the generalizability of the findings.

Reflection and Future Directions

This project gave me experience in designing a study, organizing data, and analyzing results. It also helped me learn the importance of distinguishing a visible pattern from a conclusion that can be drawn from statistical evidence. A future version of the study could include more participants, multiple speakers, different accents, different sentences, and more emotions. Comparing human detection abilities with automated detection tools would also be an interesting extension of the research.
